## Core message (one sentence)

The value of AI in research comes less from asking a chatbot better questions and more from giving it structure: standing instructions, access to the right documents and tools, and a defined workflow that a researcher still checks and owns.

## Format

- About 12 slides for 20 minutes. Use the WashU "Why Health?" template (white background, black Avenir, WashU red accent), with one idea per slide and few words.
- Three acts: why it matters (4 min), how to extend it (12 min), what to watch (4 min). Then discussion.

## Slide outline

**Act 1. Framing (about 4 min)**

1. Title: "Extending AI in research: skills, connections and workflows." Your name, BIG, Nov 20.
2. Where the group already is. Most people here use a chat assistant for drafting, summarizing or code. Ask for a show of hands to open, which also sets up the discussion.
3. Opportunities: time returned on literature scanning, first drafts, data cleaning code, and administrative writing; faster iteration on study materials.
4. Uncertainties and concerns: accuracy and fabricated citations, data privacy and IRB obligations, authorship and disclosure norms, equity of access, and the risk of deskilling junior researchers. Name these early so the rest of the talk is read with them in mind.

**Act 2. Three ways to extend AI use (about 12 min)**

5. The shift: from one-off prompts to set-ups that persist. A short diagram of prompt, then skill, then connected tools, then workflow.
6. Reusable skills. A skill is a saved set of instructions the assistant loads when a task comes up, such as a journal's formatting rules, a lab's writing style, or a codebook. Example: a writing-style skill that applies the same standards to every draft.
7. Connections through MCP. The Model Context Protocol lets the assistant read from tools you already use, such as email, calendar, Drive, PubMed or a trial registry, with your permission. Example: searching PubMed and returning a cited summary in one step.
8. Agentic workflows. The assistant carries out a multi-step task on a schedule and reports back. Example: a weekly project status table compiled from email and checked by you before it is sent.
9. What the researcher still owns: the question, the judgment, the verification, and the final text. Every output is checked before it leaves your hands.

**Act 3. Using it responsibly (about 4 min)**

10. Guardrails that work in practice: keep identifiable data out unless the tool is approved for it; verify every citation against the source; disclose AI use where journals and funders require it; keep a record of what the assistant did.
11. Where it could help this group: grant boilerplate and biosketches, systematic-review screening support, codebook-driven qualitative coding drafts, dissemination briefs from published papers. Pick one or two that fit current BIG projects.
12. Closing: "Give it structure, keep the judgment." Open the floor.

**Reserve slides**

- R1. A side-by-side of the same task done as a plain prompt and as a skill.
- R2. What an MCP connection can and cannot see, and how permissions work.
- R3. Institutional policy pointers (WashU guidance on generative AI, journal disclosure policies).
- R4. Costs and access: what is free, what needs a license.

## Discussion prompts (15 min)

- Which part of your current research workflow takes the most time for the least thinking?
- What would you need to trust an AI-assisted step enough to use it in a funded project?
- Where should the group draw the line on AI use with participant data?
